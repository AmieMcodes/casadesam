\# Quick Answer



Adults with autism and intellectual disabilities may live:



\- with parents or other relatives;

\- in their own apartment or house without formal support;

\- in their own home with scheduled or on-call support;

\- with a roommate, caregiver, or host family;

\- in a supervised apartment programme;

\- in a small group home;

\- in a staffed residential community;

\- or, when intensive medical or institutional care is necessary, in a specialised facility.



The right option is not determined by diagnosis alone.



It depends on the person’s:



\- preferences;

\- communication;

\- daily living skills;

\- safety awareness;

\- medical needs;

\- overnight needs;

\- ability to summon help;

\- sensory needs;

\- behaviour-support needs;

\- financial resources;

\- available public services;

\- and desired level of family involvement.



A person’s housing and support should also be considered separately.



An adult may rent an ordinary apartment but receive extensive daily support. Another adult may live in a provider-operated home but need relatively little direct assistance.



“Independent” should not mean “left alone without adequate support.”

\# Introduction



Parents are often asked whether their autistic or intellectually disabled child will eventually live independently.



That question can make adulthood sound like a test with only two outcomes: complete independence or failure.



Real adult housing is much more varied.



Some adults live alone but receive daily support. Some share a home with one carefully chosen roommate. Some remain with family while using outside caregivers or respite services. Others need awake overnight staff, nursing support, secure outdoor space, or a lifelong residential setting.



The most useful question is not:



> Can this person live independently?



It is:



> What combination of home, support, relationships, safeguards, and funding will allow this person to live a safe and satisfying adult life?



This article will compare the major housing arrangements families may encounter, explain their trade-offs, and provide practical questions to use when evaluating them.

\# Main Article Structure



\## H2: Housing and support are not the same thing



\### Purpose



Help readers understand one of the most important distinctions in disability housing.



The building or household is one part of the arrangement.



The support system is another.



\### Key Points



\- Housing refers to the physical home and the legal or financial arrangement attached to it.

\- Support refers to the help a person receives to live there safely.

\- A person may own or rent a private home while receiving substantial support.

\- A person may live in a staffed setting but still need family advocacy and outside services.

\- Housing may be paid through rent, family resources, public housing, vouchers, trusts, benefits, or another arrangement.

\- Support may be funded separately through Medicaid, state disability programmes, private payment, family caregiving, or nonprofit services.

\- Families should never assume that receiving housing automatically includes all necessary support.

\- Families should never assume that a service programme pays the rent unless that is explicitly stated.

\- CMS Home and Community-Based Services

\- HUD Section 811

\- State-specific examples only if clearly labelled as examples

\## H2: Living with parents or relatives



\### Purpose



Treat family care as a genuine housing model rather than merely an absence of planning.



\### Key Points



\- Many adults with developmental disabilities continue living with family.

\- This arrangement may offer familiarity, consistency, trusted relationships, and a highly individualised environment.

\- It may work well for many years.

\- The family may still use paid caregivers, personal assistance, respite, day services, transportation, or home modifications.

\- Living with family does not necessarily mean the adult receives less choice or has no adult life.

\- The arrangement becomes fragile when it depends entirely on one aging, ill, or exhausted caregiver.

\- Families need written backup plans.

\- Families should consider what happens during hospitalisation, death, financial crisis, housing loss, or caregiver burnout.

\- Future planning should begin before an emergency forces a sudden move.



\### Sources Needed



\- CMS material concerning adults with I/DD and aging caregivers

\- Administration for Community Living caregiver and community-resource information

\## H2: Independent living without formal residential support



\### Purpose



Explain true independent housing without treating it as the highest or only desirable goal.



\### Key Points



\- The adult may rent or own an apartment or house.

\- They may live alone, with a partner, or with a roommate.

\- They manage most daily tasks without a formal residential provider.

\- Informal help may still come from family, friends, neighbours, technology, transportation services, or community organisations.

\- This model may work for adults who can recognise emergencies, seek help, manage basic routines, and remain safe between visits.

\- Some adults appear independent until illness, exploitation, a missed payment, medication error, household problem, or unexpected change occurs.

\- Families should assess actual functioning rather than relying only on verbal ability, academic skills, or a desire for privacy.

\- Independence may increase gradually over time.

\- A trial apartment or staged transition may provide better information than assumptions.



\### Sources Needed



\- Administration for Community Living

\- Local Centers for Independent Living where discussed

\- No universal eligibility claims



\---



\## H2: Supported living in a private apartment or house



\### Purpose



Explain that an adult may control or occupy their own home while receiving scheduled support.



\### Key Points



\- The person usually lives in a home or apartment rather than a provider-operated group residence.

\- Support may include help with meals, cleaning, budgeting, transportation, appointments, communication, medication routines, personal care, or community activities.

\- Staff may visit at scheduled times.

\- Some programmes provide on-call help.

\- Some provide overnight support, while others do not.

\- Supported living may range from a few hours each week to extensive daily assistance.

\- The exact definition varies by state and provider.

\- Families must verify who responds between staff visits.

\- Families must determine whether the person can summon help.

\- Housing tenancy and support-provider agreements should be understood separately.

\- A loss of service funding should not automatically be assumed to mean the person can remain safely in the home.



\### Sources Needed



\- CMS HCBS

\- State Medicaid or developmental-disability agency examples

\- Avoid presenting one state’s definition as universal



\---



\## H2: Shared living, host homes, and adult foster arrangements



\### Purpose



Explain household-based arrangements that fall between living with relatives and living in a group home.



\### Key Points



\- The adult may live with a paid caregiver, host family, or household provider.

\- The terminology varies widely.

\- The setting may be the caregiver’s home, the disabled adult’s home, or a shared home.

\- This model can offer continuity, family-style routines, and a smaller household.

\- Success depends heavily on compatibility, boundaries, oversight, relief coverage, and the quality of the individual caregiver.

\- The adult needs meaningful choice and a way to report concerns.

\- Families should ask what happens when the host becomes ill, moves, retires, or ends the arrangement.

\- Respite and backup staffing are essential.

\- The household should not become isolated from outside oversight.



\### Sources Needed



\- State programme pages used only as examples

\- Relevant licensing information by location

\- No universal definition



\---



\## H2: Supervised apartment programmes



\### Purpose



Explain apartment-based programmes with nearby or shared staffing.



\### Key Points



\- Residents may have individual apartments or share with one or more roommates.

\- Staff may be located in the building, in a nearby apartment, or available during specific hours.

\- Programmes may teach or support daily living skills.

\- The model may provide more privacy than a traditional group home.

\- It may be suitable for adults who need regular assistance but not constant direct supervision.

\- “Supervised” does not necessarily mean awake staff are present all night.

\- Families should ask how quickly staff can respond.

\- Families should ask whether residents can remain if their needs increase.

\- The resident’s lease, programme participation, and service eligibility may be separate matters.



\### Sources Needed



\- State or local programme materials if examples are included

\- CMS HCBS principles



\---



\## H2: Group homes



\### Purpose



Explain group homes without describing every provider-operated residence as identical.



\### Key Points



\- A group home is generally a residence shared by several disabled adults with paid support.

\- Home size, staffing, licensing, resident rights, and service intensity vary.

\- Some homes provide intermittent support.

\- Others are staffed continuously.

\- Some have awake overnight staff.

\- Others rely on sleep staff or on-call coverage.

\- Group homes may provide companionship, shared routines, staff availability, and coordinated support.

\- Challenges may include staff turnover, incompatible roommates, limited privacy, fixed schedules, transportation constraints, and changing house rules.

\- Families should evaluate the actual home rather than the label.

\- A small home can still feel institutional if residents have little choice.

\- A provider-operated home can still feel personal and respectful when residents have privacy, voice, stable relationships, and ordinary household life.



\### Sources Needed



\- CMS HCBS settings requirements

\- State licensing or provider information where relevant

\- Future internal article: What Is a Group Home for Adults With Autism?



\---



\## H2: Staffed residential communities



\### Purpose



Explain communities that include multiple homes or residences with shared support systems.



\### Key Points



\- Residents may live in individual homes, apartments, cottages, or small shared houses.

\- The community may provide central staffing, transportation, recreation, meals, medical coordination, security, or emergency coverage.

\- A larger organisation may offer more backup when one staff member is unavailable.

\- Residents may have access to a broader social community.

\- The setting should still preserve privacy, adult choice, community access, and individual routines.

\- Families should determine whether the model is genuinely a home or primarily a programme.

\- Families should ask whether participation in work, classes, worship, recreation, or group meals is optional.

\- Long-term financial stability is particularly important when an organisation promises lifelong care.

\- The admissions policy must make clear what happens if support needs increase.



\### Sources Needed



\- CMS HCBS settings principles

\- Casa de SAM vision document for Casa de SAM-specific statements

\- Do not imply that all residential communities operate the same way



\---



\## H2: Intentional communities, farmsteads, and campus-style models



\### Purpose



Address models families often encounter online without automatically praising or condemning them.



\### Key Points



\- These settings may be organised around shared community life, agriculture, recreation, faith, employment, or another central purpose.

\- Some residents and families value space, routine, nature, familiarity, and strong social connections.

\- Critics may be concerned about isolation, limited integration, compulsory work, or institutional features.

\- The label alone does not determine quality.

\- Families should examine resident choice, freedom of movement, access to the wider community, employment expectations, transportation, privacy, and decision-making rights.

\- Work should not be required as a condition of belonging.

\- Residents should not be expected to earn their place.

\- The environment should adapt to residents rather than forcing every resident into one lifestyle.



\### Sources Needed



\- CMS HCBS settings requirements

\- Disability-rights perspectives

\- Casa de SAM philosophy only where explicitly identified as Casa de SAM’s approach

\## H2: Intermediate care facilities and specialised institutional care



\### Purpose



Explain that some institutional options remain part of the system without presenting them as ordinary group homes.



\### Key Points



\- Intermediate Care Facilities for Individuals with Intellectual Disabilities are a Medicaid institutional benefit.

\- They provide comprehensive and individualised health and rehabilitation services.

\- Availability, size, admission criteria, and operation vary.

\- Some people may require intensive services that their local community system cannot safely provide.

\- Families should understand whether a proposed placement is classified as HCBS, an ICF/IID, a nursing facility, or another licensed setting.

\- The classification can affect rights, funding, services, and regulatory oversight.

\- A nursing facility should not be treated as the default housing solution solely because an adult has a developmental disability.

\- Medical need, not caregiver scarcity alone, should guide nursing-level placement.



\### Sources Needed



\- CMS Intermediate Care Facilities for Individuals with Intellectual Disabilities

\- CMS Institutional Long-Term Care

\- Location-specific professional guidance

\# Definitions and Distinctions



\## Housing



The physical place where someone lives and the legal or financial arrangement governing that home.



Examples include:



\- a family-owned home;

\- a rented apartment;

\- a subsidised apartment;

\- a provider-operated house;

\- or a room in a licensed facility.



\## Residential support



Paid or organised assistance that helps someone live safely in their home.



This may include:



\- personal care;

\- meal preparation;

\- medication support;

\- communication assistance;

\- budgeting;

\- transportation;

\- housekeeping;

\- behavioural support;

\- supervision;

\- emergency response;

\- or overnight care.



\## Independent living



Living with control over one’s own home and daily life.



It should not automatically be interpreted as receiving no help.



A person can make meaningful choices and direct their life while using substantial assistance.



\## Supported living



A broad term for an arrangement in which a disabled adult lives in a home or apartment while receiving services that help them maintain that living arrangement.



Its legal and programme-specific definition varies by location.



\## Group home



A shared residence where several disabled adults live and receive paid support.



The term does not reveal:



\- the number of residents;

\- the staffing ratio;

\- whether staff are awake overnight;

\- the quality of the home;

\- the degree of resident choice;

\- or what happens if needs increase.



\## Residential community



A broader setting containing more than one residence or household with shared organisational support.



It may include houses, apartments, cottages, central facilities, staff, transportation, and community activities.



\## HCBS



Home and Community-Based Services are Medicaid services intended to help eligible people receive long-term services and supports in homes and community settings rather than institutional settings.



States design and administer their programmes within federal requirements, so available services and names differ.



\## ICF/IID



An Intermediate Care Facility for Individuals with Intellectual Disabilities is an institutional Medicaid service category that provides health and rehabilitation services to eligible individuals.



It is distinct from ordinary rental housing, supported living, or most HCBS group-home arrangements.

\# Who This Option May Suit



There is no single housing option represented by this article, so this section should instead explain how to match an arrangement to a person.



The best option is one that can reliably meet the person’s needs without unnecessarily restricting their life.



Families should consider:



\- Where does the person want to live?

\- What kind of household feels comfortable?

\- How much privacy do they want?

\- How do they communicate preferences or distress?

\- Can they recognise danger?

\- Can they call, text, use a device, activate an alarm, or otherwise summon help?

\- Can they remain safely alone?

\- For how long?

\- Do they sleep through the night?

\- Do they wander or leave unexpectedly?

\- Do they need help with bathing, dressing, toileting, eating, or mobility?

\- Do they need medication administration or nursing care?

\- Do they experience seizures, diabetes, choking risks, or other urgent medical needs?

\- What sensory conditions help them remain regulated?

\- How do they respond to unfamiliar staff or roommates?

\- Do they need predictable routines?

\- What transportation is available?

\- How involved does the person want family to remain?

\- Can the housing continue if needs increase?

\- Is there reliable emergency and backup staffing?



The answer may change over time.



A person might begin in the family home, move to a supported apartment, and later need overnight staffing.



Another person may need 24-hour support from the beginning but gain more privacy and decision-making over time.

\# Who May Need Something Different



A housing arrangement may be unsafe or unsustainable when:



\- the person cannot summon help and is left alone;

\- staff are present for fewer hours than the person actually needs;

\- overnight risks are not addressed;

\- medication support is unreliable;

\- the programme cannot manage seizures, choking, diabetes, mobility, or other medical needs;

\- the person regularly leaves the property without awareness of danger;

\- the home depends entirely on one caregiver with no backup;

\- roommates create ongoing fear, conflict, or sensory distress;

\- the person is unable to communicate concerns to anyone outside the home;

\- staffing turnover prevents stable relationships;

\- transportation makes community participation unrealistic;

\- the family must quietly provide unpaid support that the programme claims to cover;

\- the adult can be discharged when needs increase;

\- or the financial arrangement cannot realistically continue.



A more restrictive setting is not automatically safer.



A less restrictive setting is not automatically more respectful.



The real question is whether the arrangement provides enough support while preserving as much freedom, privacy, connection, and adult choice as possible.

\# Trade-Offs and Limitations



\## Privacy versus immediate support



A private apartment may offer control, quiet, and dignity.



It may also leave a person without immediate assistance during an emergency.



A staffed home may offer faster support but less privacy.



Families should ask what support is physically present, what is merely on call, and how long response usually takes.



\## Familiarity versus long-term fragility



Living with family may provide the most individualised and loving environment.



It can also depend on one caregiver who will not always be available.



The goal is not to force an early move.



The goal is to build enough outside support that a future transition does not begin with a crisis.



\## Small household versus organisational backup



A host home or tiny shared residence may feel personal and consistent.



A larger organisation may have more staff, transportation, administrative capacity, and emergency backup.



The right balance depends on the actual people and systems involved.



\## Choice versus programme rules



Some programmes coordinate meals, transportation, appointments, recreation, and staffing efficiently.



Efficiency can become control when residents have little say over:



\- when they wake;

\- what they eat;

\- where they go;

\- who enters their room;

\- which activities they attend;

\- or whom they live with.



Families should distinguish between necessary safety limits and rules created for staff convenience.



\## Stability versus changing needs



A home may work beautifully at age 22 but not at age 50.



Families should ask:



\- Can support hours increase?

\- Can nursing be added?

\- Can the home accommodate mobility changes?

\- Can the resident remain through illness or dementia?

\- What circumstances can lead to discharge?

\- Who helps locate another home if the placement ends?



\## Affordability versus availability



The least expensive model on paper may require unavailable family labour.



A publicly funded option may have a lengthy waiting list.



A private arrangement may be flexible but financially unsustainable.



Families need to compare:



\- rent;

\- food;

\- utilities;

\- transportation;

\- staffing;

\- supervision;

\- personal care;

\- medical support;

\- administration;

\- emergency coverage;

\- and unpaid family involvement.

\# Common Misconceptions



\## Misconception One



\*\*Claim:\*\* Adults either live independently or live in a group home.



\*\*Reality:\*\* Housing exists along a broad spectrum. An adult may live with family, rent an apartment with support, share a home with a caregiver, live in a supervised apartment programme, join a small staffed household, or live in a broader residential community.



\## Misconception Two



\*\*Claim:\*\* Supported living means someone checks in occasionally.



\*\*Reality:\*\* Support intensity varies. Some people receive only a few scheduled hours. Others receive daily personal assistance, extensive supervision, or overnight help. Families must ask exactly what support is included.



\## Misconception Three



\*\*Claim:\*\* A person who speaks well can safely live alone.



\*\*Reality:\*\* Speech does not establish emergency judgment, financial safety, medication management, resistance to exploitation, household skills, or the ability to seek help.



\## Misconception Four



\*\*Claim:\*\* A nonspeaking adult cannot make housing choices.



\*\*Reality:\*\* Communication is broader than speech. Preferences may be expressed through augmentative communication, behaviour, routines, gestures, facial expression, trial visits, and careful observation.



\## Misconception Five



\*\*Claim:\*\* A smaller home is always better.



\*\*Reality:\*\* Home size matters, but so do staffing, stability, privacy, compatibility, oversight, transportation, and the resident’s preferences. A tiny setting with no backup may be less safe than a larger organisation with strong individualised care.



\## Misconception Six



\*\*Claim:\*\* Once a programme accepts someone, they can remain there for life.



\*\*Reality:\*\* Admission does not always guarantee lifelong placement. Families must ask about discharge policies, increasing support needs, hospitalisation, behavioural crises, nonpayment, medical changes, and provider closure.



\## Misconception Seven



\*\*Claim:\*\* Moving away from family automatically creates independence.



\*\*Reality:\*\* A different address does not necessarily create choice, skills, safety, belonging, or control. Independence should be measured by the person’s real life, not merely by whether they live with parents.

\# Practical Questions to Ask



\## About the home



\- Who owns or leases the home?

\- Does the resident have a lease or residency agreement?

\- Does the resident have a private bedroom?

\- Can the resident lock their bedroom?

\- Who may enter the resident’s room, and under what circumstances?

\- Can the resident choose décor, furniture, meals, and daily routines?

\- Are visitors allowed?

\- Can family stay overnight?

\- How are roommates selected?

\- What happens when roommates are incompatible?

\- Is the home physically accessible?

\- Is there safe outdoor space?

\- How are sensory needs accommodated?



\## About staffing



\- Is staff physically present 24 hours a day?

\- Is overnight staff awake, asleep, nearby, or on call?

\- What is the usual response time during an emergency?

\- How many residents does each staff member support?

\- What training is required?

\- Are background checks required?

\- How often does staff turnover occur?

\- Can the resident request a different staff member?

\- What happens when scheduled staff do not arrive?

\- Who provides backup during illness, holidays, or emergencies?

\- Are volunteers ever alone with residents?



\## About health and safety



\- Who manages medications?

\- Can staff respond to seizures, choking, diabetes, allergies, or other medical needs?

\- Is nursing available?

\- How are hospital visits handled?

\- Who communicates with doctors?

\- What happens if the resident leaves unexpectedly?

\- What safety technology is used?

\- Are safety tools discreet and respectful?

\- How are incidents documented?

\- When and how is family notified?

\- Are behavioural crises handled without punishment or unnecessary police involvement?



\## About rights and daily life



\- Can residents choose whether to participate in activities?

\- Is employment voluntary?

\- Are classes voluntary?

\- Can residents attend their chosen church or community activities?

\- Can residents spend time alone?

\- Can residents maintain friendships and relationships?

\- How are sexuality and adult relationships addressed?

\- Can residents disagree with staff?

\- Is there an independent complaint process?

\- Who advocates for residents without active family involvement?



\## About money and continuation



\- What does the monthly payment cover?

\- What costs are separate?

\- Is public funding accepted?

\- Does funding pay for housing, support, or both?

\- What happens if benefits are interrupted?

\- Can fees increase?

\- Is there a written fee schedule?

\- Is there an emergency reserve?

\- What happens if the provider closes?

\- Under what circumstances can a resident be discharged?

\- Can the person remain if medical or support needs increase?

\- Is there a transition obligation if the placement ends?

\# What You Can Do This Week



\## 1. Write a one-page support snapshot



List the assistance the adult currently receives during a normal day.



Include:



\- waking;

\- dressing;

\- toileting;

\- meals;

\- medication;

\- communication;

\- transportation;

\- appointments;

\- safety;

\- recreation;

\- emotional regulation;

\- bedtime;

\- and overnight needs.



Do not write what the person “should” be able to do.



Write what actually happens now.



\## 2. Separate housing needs from support needs



Create two columns.



In the first, list what the physical home must provide.



Examples:



\- quiet bedroom;

\- fenced outdoor space;

\- accessible bathroom;

\- public transportation;

\- room for overnight family;

\- or proximity to medical care.



In the second, list the human support required.



Examples:



\- meal preparation;

\- personal care;

\- medication administration;

\- awake overnight supervision;

\- transportation;

\- communication assistance;

\- or emergency response.



This makes it easier to compare arrangements accurately.



\## 3. Identify the first local doorway



Search for one of the following in your area:



\- state developmental-disability agency;

\- Medicaid HCBS waiver office;

\- Center for Independent Living;

\- disability resource centre;

\- public housing authority;

\- Section 811 housing;

\- disability legal-aid office;

\- or disability information and referral service.



The goal is not to master the entire system.



The goal is to identify the agency that can explain the next local step.



\## 4. Ask about waiting lists now



Do not assume the person is automatically enrolled because they have a diagnosis.



Ask:



\- Is there a formal application?

\- Is there a waiting list?

\- Can a child or teenager join before adulthood?

\- Does the person need Medicaid eligibility?

\- Is a functional assessment required?

\- Must the person meet an institutional level of care?

\- How often must information be updated?



Joining a waiting list does not commit the family to using that service.



It preserves future options.



\## 5. Name one emergency backup person



Write down who could step in tonight if the primary caregiver were unexpectedly hospitalised.



If there is no one, write that down honestly.



That gap should become a planning priority.



The family does not need to solve the entire future this week. The goal is to make the current situation visible and take one useful next step.

\*\*Name:\*\* Medicaid Home and Community-Based Services



\*\*Link:\*\* https://www.medicaid.gov/medicaid/home-community-based-services



\*\*Use:\*\* Explains the federal framework that allows eligible Medicaid beneficiaries to receive long-term services and supports in homes and community settings rather than institutional settings.



Readers should then locate their own state’s Medicaid and developmental-disability programmes because services and eligibility differ.



\## CMS: HCBS 1915(c) Waivers



\*\*Name:\*\* Home and Community-Based Services 1915(c)



\*\*Link:\*\* https://www.medicaid.gov/medicaid/home-community-based-services/home-community-based-services-authorities/home-community-based-services-1915c



\*\*Use:\*\* Explains the federal waiver authority states commonly use to provide services to people who would otherwise require institutional levels of care.



It is useful background, but readers must consult their state for actual waiver names, waiting lists, and covered services.



\## CMS: Intermediate Care Facilities for Individuals with Intellectual Disabilities



\*\*Name:\*\* Intermediate Care Facilities for Individuals with Intellectual Disabilities



\*\*Link:\*\* https://www.medicaid.gov/medicaid/long-term-services-supports/institutional-long-term-care/intermediate-care-facilities-individuals-intellectual-disability



\*\*Use:\*\* Explains the federal Medicaid institutional benefit known as ICF/IID.



This helps families distinguish institutional care from home and community-based residential services.



\## HUD: Section 811 Supportive Housing for Persons with Disabilities



\*\*Name:\*\* Section 811 Supportive Housing for Persons with Disabilities



\*\*Link:\*\* https://www.hud.gov/hud-partners/multifamily-grants-section811ptl



\*\*Use:\*\* Provides official information about a federal programme supporting multifamily housing for very low-income people with disabilities.



Availability is local and limited. Readers should contact their local housing authority or HUD office rather than assuming a unit is immediately available.



\## Disability Information and Access Locator



\*\*Name:\*\* DIAL



\*\*Link:\*\* https://dial.acl.gov/home



\*\*Use:\*\* Helps people with disabilities and families locate state and local organisations connected with independent living, housing, transportation, legal assistance, and other community services.



\## Administration for Community Living Directories



\*\*Name:\*\* Administration for Community Living Housing and Services Directories



\*\*Link:\*\* https://acl.gov/HousingAndServices/Directories



\*\*Use:\*\* Provides directories and referral pathways for community-living services and disability organisations.



\## Local Center for Independent Living



\*\*Name:\*\* Center for Independent Living



\*\*Link:\*\* Use DIAL or the Administration for Community Living directory to locate the nearest centre.



\*\*Use:\*\* Centers for Independent Living are community-based organisations led by people with disabilities. Depending on the centre, they may provide information, peer support, advocacy, skills training, transition assistance, and referrals.



\## State developmental-disability agency



\*\*Name:\*\* State Intellectual or Developmental Disabilities Agency



\*\*Link:\*\* Varies by state.



\*\*Use:\*\* This is often the most important entry point for assessments, waiver programmes, case management, service coordination, residential options, and waiting-list information.



Search for the state name plus “developmental disabilities services.”

\# Personal Perspective



\## Suggested Heading



The question underneath the housing question



\## Draft Direction



For many parents, including me, housing is not an abstract policy topic.



It is part of the question:



> What will happen to my special needs child when I die?



My son Sam is still a child. He is nonspeaking and needs significant support. I cannot predict exactly what his abilities, health, communication, or daily life will look like decades from now.



I also do not want to pretend that adulthood has only one successful shape.



My goal is not to prove that Sam can live with the least help possible.



My goal is for him to have a safe, joyful, ordinary adult life in a home where people know him well, respect his communication, notice when something is wrong, and continue caring for him after I am gone.



That requires more than choosing a building.



It requires:



\- dependable people;

\- long-term funding;

\- meaningful oversight;

\- family connection;

\- emergency planning;

\- privacy;

\- community;

\- and a promise that increasing needs will not make someone disposable.



Families should not be pressured into one universal model.



They should be given enough honest information to recognise what their own family member needs.

\# About Casa de SAM



Casa de SAM is a developing vision for a lifelong residential community in Paraguay for adults with developmental disabilities who need ongoing support.



We believe joyful living is inherently valuable.



Our promise is that once a resident is fully accepted, Casa de SAM will care for them for life.



Casa de SAM is being planned as a community of real homes rather than one large institution. Residents will not be required to work, attend classes, or participate in activities to earn their place. The environment, staffing, routines, and safety systems will be designed around the individuals who live there.



The project is currently in its planning and organisational stage, with a long-term goal of opening in 2035.



Casa de SAM is not yet a registered nonprofit and is not currently accepting donations.



Do not hard-code CTA language here. CTA components are managed separately.

\# Disclaimer



This article was written in July 2026 and provides general educational information, primarily for families in the United States.



Housing terminology, Medicaid programmes, waiver services, licensing categories, funding, eligibility requirements, tenant protections, and agency procedures vary by state and individual circumstances and can change over time.



The availability of a programme does not establish that a particular person is eligible or that housing is currently available.



Casa de SAM will make every reasonable effort to review and update this article, but readers should confirm current information directly with state developmental-disability agencies, Medicaid offices, housing authorities, qualified attorneys, benefits professionals, and prospective service providers.
