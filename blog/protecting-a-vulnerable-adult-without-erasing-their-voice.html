LEGAL RIGHTS AND ADULT DECISION-MAKING · OCTOBER 9, 2026

How Families Can Protect a Vulnerable Adult Without Erasing Their Voice

Safety and self-determination are not opposing goals. Learn how to listen, document risk, and build meaningful safeguards.

There is a fear many parents of profoundly disabled children carry quietly: "If I do not make every decision, who will keep my child safe?" It is a serious question. Exploitation, medical emergencies, unsafe environments, and unreliable caregivers are real risks for some adults with significant disabilities.

The answer cannot be to pretend those risks do not exist. But it also cannot be to treat protection as a permanent waiver of someone's preferences. A rights-respecting plan asks what danger is being addressed, how the adult can participate, and whether the proposed restriction goes further than necessary.

An adult can require 24-hour support and still deserve choices about clothing, activities, relationships, food, private space, and the ordinary rhythm of the day. Supporting Choice When an Adult Cannot Live Completely Independently explains why needing assistance does not erase that everyday authority.

Hearing a person who does not speak

Speech is one communication method, not the definition of having a voice. People may use AAC devices, picture boards, signs, gestures, facial expression, movement, familiar routines, or a combination. The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association's AAC guidance describes assessment and supports that can expand functional communication.

The difficult part is being honest about interpretation. Turning toward a familiar person may suggest a preference; resisting a particular medical procedure may signal fear, pain, sensory distress, or refusal. It does not follow that a caregiver can interpret every movement with certainty. Staff should record the situation, their observations, the alternatives they offered, and what happened next.

This distinction matters legally and ethically. Everyday preference, meaningful participation, informed consent, and authorization for a high-stakes decision are related but not interchangeable. When a choice carries serious risk, families should seek appropriate communication assessment and follow the law governing consent in that jurisdiction.

Start with the smallest workable protection

Suppose a person darts toward traffic. The danger is immediate and substantial. A practical response might include a trained companion, safer walking routes, an individualized supervision plan, environmental design, and emergency procedures. The objective is not to stop the person from ever leaving home.

Samuel and I have a favorite early-morning walking route here in San Lorenzo, Paraguay. Along the path, beside a busy two-lane street, is a tiny playground with just a couple of pieces of equipment. On one sunrise walk, Sam stopped to play on a blue spring rider. In the photograph, the path is in the foreground, the toy is in the middle, and a guardrail stands right behind it, with traffic on the other side.

I keep coming back to that guardrail. It addresses a real, identifiable risk without deciding which toy Sam can choose or how he should enjoy it. Good safeguards should work that way wherever possible: protect a person's opportunity to participate, rather than using the existence of a hazard as a reason to remove every opportunity.

Suppose instead that a person refuses to wear a sweater. Unless there is a specific health or safety reason, insisting may protect adult expectations rather than the disabled person. A warmer garment in a different texture, a delayed departure, or an informed choice may solve the problem without a power struggle.

These examples illustrate a useful test: What specific harm are we preventing, what evidence supports the restriction, what alternative has been tried, and when will we reconsider? The answer should be recorded, not left as an unwritten staff habit.

The U.S. Department of Justice explains that broad guardianship can displace important rights and should be considered alongside narrower options. That U.S. framework does not dictate Paraguayan law, but the question of proportionality travels across settings.

A refusal deserves investigation, not automatic obedience or automatic force

Consider toothbrushing, a meal, or a medical exam. A non-speaking adult's resistance may be an important warning. It might point to a painful tooth, an intolerable texture, a traumatic experience, an unfamiliar staff member, or a choice the person is trying to communicate.

A useful care sequence is to pause when safe, check for urgent medical problems, change one feature of the task, offer accessible choices, and document what reduces distress. If an essential intervention cannot safely wait, clinicians and the legally authorized decision process must weigh the risk and available alternatives. Emergency circumstances require a different response from routine care.

The point is neither "refusal always wins" nor "the professional always wins." It is that a person should not lose the right to be taken seriously because their communication is difficult to interpret.

Casa de SAM's Full Vision Document explicitly treats refusal as meaningful information and calls for curiosity, adaptation, and the least traumatic safe response.

Protect privacy as well as physical safety

Well-intentioned oversight can become invasive. A family group chat containing intimate care details, cameras in personal spaces, or routine publication of a disabled adult's distress may be easy for caregivers and humiliating for the person.

A better support plan distinguishes what staff must know from what friends, visitors, donors, or social-media followers may see. Share the minimum information needed for the task, secure sensitive records, create private places to receive care, and explain how a resident or trusted advocate can challenge inappropriate disclosure.

Parents may need more detailed information during a health crisis; a paid caregiver may need a particular medication instruction. Neither necessarily needs access to every personal detail. Legal rights to information vary by role and jurisdiction.

Build safeguards against the people providing protection

Supporters and service providers are human. They can misunderstand, burn out, develop conflicts of interest, or—in serious cases—exploit people who rely on them. A plan is stronger when it does not require one person to be both the decision-maker, service provider, treasurer, and sole complaint investigator.

The National Council on Disability documented concerns about lost rights and weak oversight in guardianship systems. Its report offers reasons to scrutinize how authority is exercised, while not establishing that families must reject every legal protection.

Practical safeguards include:

  • A clear record of who has which authority, with copies of the relevant current documents.
  • An independent person to whom the adult or a supporter can report concerns.
  • Regular reviews of restrictions, not just staff reviews of the person's behavior.
  • Separate financial records and conflict-of-interest checks when someone benefits from a decision.
  • A communication profile that travels with the person during moves, hospitalizations, or staff changes.

The law should protect choice, not simply certify control

The international rights framework matters here. Article 12 of the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities recognizes equal legal capacity and calls for safeguards that respect the person's rights, will, and preferences while guarding against abuse and undue influence.

Paraguay took a significant step through Law No. 7371/2025, effective July 16, 2025. The Judicial Branch describes a move away from older disability-related declarations of incapacity and curatorship toward personalized supports. Families and future providers should get current local advice about how those supports are established and safeguarded in practice.

In U.S. states, rules differ. The American Bar Association advocates considering less restrictive options, including supported decision-making, rather than equating disability with an inability to decide. Whether a person can use a particular agreement, proxy, or court process still depends on the facts and governing law.

For a closer comparison of the tools themselves, see Guardianship, Supported Decision-Making, and Adult Disability. That companion article is part of this same three-post series.

What this looks like in a family's ordinary life

My son Samuel does not use spoken language. Yet he makes choices all the time: what he wants to eat, which activity interests him, when he wants to keep moving, and what he has no interest in doing. Sometimes I understand immediately; sometimes I am still learning.

I do not pretend those everyday choices answer every future legal question. They remind me that a system designed to protect him must be capable of listening to him. If the people caring for him in adulthood recognize only the decisions made by adults around him, then something essential has been lost—even if every form is signed.

Samuel is one child, not a model for every autistic person. The correct supports and safeguards will differ, and they may change over his lifetime.

What You Can Do This Week

  • Watch one ordinary choice without directing it. Record the options shown, the adult's response, your interpretation, and any uncertainty.
  • Choose one safety restriction currently in use. Write down the hazard, who authorized the restriction, when it began, and a less restrictive alternative to test safely.
  • Identify who outside the immediate caregiving team would hear a complaint or notice exploitation.
  • Review how medical, personal-care, and photo information is stored and shared. Remove access that people do not need.
  • Ask a speech-language professional or disability advocate what communication supports could make one recurring decision more accessible.

Long-Term Questions to Consider

How will a newly hired caregiver learn the person's communication? Who may challenge a restriction imposed in an emergency? What happens when a parent disagrees with the adult's expressed preference? How will the adult's private relationships be protected? Who can independently examine a safety incident or financial concern?

Casa de SAM's Position

Our vision includes a Resident Bill of Rights because a home should never have to choose between meaningful safety and human dignity. We want residents to move, rest, communicate, refuse, enjoy relationships, and have personal space while receiving the level of support their actual needs require.

A plan that prevents harm but erases the person is unfinished. A plan that celebrates choice while neglecting foreseeable danger is unfinished, too. Casa de SAM is still in development; we must build the staffing, accountability, resident communication practices, and lawful safeguards needed to make these promises real.

About Casa de SAM

Casa de SAM is a developing vision for a lifelong residential community in Paraguay for adults with developmental disabilities who need substantial daily support. Its founding principles center safety, dignity, joyful daily life, and lasting belonging after full acceptance.

Casa de SAM is not yet an operating nonprofit or residential provider. We are researching and developing the legal, financial, staffing, and oversight structures needed to build the vision responsibly.
