LIFELONG CARE & FUTURE PLANNING · SEPTEMBER 25, 2026

A Parent’s Guide to Planning for Lifelong Disability Care

A useful plan connects the person, the people, the money, and the everyday care. It also explains what happens when one part fails.

How do you plan for a child who may need support for the rest of their life, especially when you cannot know how long you will be able to provide it?

Begin by separating the work into pieces: understand the person’s daily life, identify who will provide care, establish appropriate decision-making authority, investigate funding, consider housing, and prepare for emergencies. Then connect those pieces. A will cannot cover a night shift. A trusted relative cannot spend money they cannot legally access. A beautiful home cannot provide care without staff.

You do not need a final answer to every question before you begin. You need an honest account of what exists, what remains uncertain, and who will take the next step.

This guide is for families planning around substantial lifelong support needs. It does not assume every autistic person needs the same arrangement. For the underlying concern, start with who will care for my autistic child when I die?

Start with a life worth protecting

Before choosing a provider or legal document, describe what makes the person’s life recognizably theirs. Include people they seek out, places they enjoy, foods they prefer, how they rest, what they avoid, and what helps them feel secure. Record their own words, communication choices, and repeated preferences. If a preference is your interpretation, label it that way.

Next, write an ordinary day from waking through bedtime. Include the quiet work: preparing familiar food, noticing discomfort, accompanying someone near traffic, cleaning equipment, managing appointments, and helping them recover after a difficult outing. State whether each task needs a reminder, nearby supervision, direct assistance, or a particular professional skill.

For example, “can get dressed” may conceal that someone else chooses weather-appropriate clothes, notices when clothing is uncomfortable, and supplies clean replacements. A future caregiver needs the full picture, including abilities and the help surrounding them.

The photograph here came from an early-morning walk. We set out while it was still dark, and made room for a playground stop because Sam likes parks and playgrounds. That is a detail I want future caregivers to understand: the walk is part of our rhythm, but the rhythm can bend around what he enjoys. A care plan should preserve that flexibility instead of turning familiarity into a rigid timetable.

Research helps explain why planning gets postponed

In a 2018 web survey of 388 parents, Meghan Burke, Catherine Arnold, and Aleksa Owen identified barriers including limited services, finances, lack of time, emotional difficulty, and unavailable family caregivers. This was a self-report survey, not a test proving that a particular planning method works. It helps explain why telling families to “just plan ahead” misses real obstacles. Read the study abstract.

Our practical response is to divide the work into decisions that can be made separately while checking how they depend on one another.

Build three time horizons into the plan

Tonight: If the primary caregiver is unexpectedly unavailable, who can arrive, enter the home, and provide safe care? Do they know the routine and have lawful access to essential information?

The next year: Which decisions, applications, introductions, and practice visits can reduce dependence on one caregiver? This may mean developing respite support, updating documents, or beginning an adult-service application.

The longer future: Where could the person live, who would oversee care, and how would the arrangement survive aging, staff turnover, changes in health, or the death of a key supporter?

Do not make the emergency plan wait for the permanent housing decision. If you want to understand why this work begins before a crisis, read why families need to begin lifelong planning earlier.

Separate the jobs that a parent currently combines

One parent may provide personal care, arrange transportation, manage benefits, supervise paid support, authorize treatment where legally permitted, and notice when something is wrong. Those are different jobs. Write them separately before asking someone to “take over.”

  • Daily care: meals, personal care, supervision, communication, recreation, and appointments.
  • Coordination: organizing the schedule, maintaining records, and following up when services fail.
  • Legal decisions: acting only within authority established by the applicable law and documents.
  • Money management: administering funds, paying bills, maintaining records, and protecting benefits.
  • Independent oversight: keeping a relationship with the person and checking whether care is actually good.

A sibling may willingly maintain a close relationship while being unable to provide daily personal care. A professional may administer a trust without knowing which music helps someone settle. A plan can distribute responsibility without treating affection as an unlimited staffing commitment. Ask each person what they can realistically agree to do, and name a backup.

Match legal authority to the actual decision

A support profile or letter of intent records knowledge and wishes; it does not by itself create legal authority. Ask a qualified lawyer in the relevant jurisdiction which arrangements fit the person’s decision-making abilities and which decisions need formal representation.

Texas example: Estates Code Chapter 1357 allows voluntary supported decision-making agreements under which a supporter helps an adult understand information and communicate decisions, without deciding for the adult. The statute describes an alternative to guardianship for adults who need assistance but are not considered incapacitated for guardianship purposes. It is not a substitute decision-making document for every adult with profound support needs. Read Chapter 1357, especially §§1357.003 and 1357.051.

Even when formal representation is necessary, document how the person will participate, express refusal, and influence everyday choices. Someone who cannot understand a complicated financial agreement may still have clear preferences about clothing, visitors, activities, or where to sit.

Federal benefits example: Social Security appoints representative payees to manage benefits for people who cannot manage them. A power of attorney or joint bank account does not itself make someone a payee. Ask SSA how benefit management would continue if the current payee could no longer serve. SSA explains the separate appointment process.

Plan the funding and the handoff together

List recurring costs before choosing a savings target: housing, paid support, food, transportation, health care, equipment, communication, personal supplies, and enjoyable activities. Record unpaid care hours separately. Those hours do not become free simply because a family has always supplied them.

U.S. trust example: SSA explains that trusts can count as resources for SSI, while certain special-needs and pooled trusts receive exceptions. Distributions can also affect payments, and Medicaid treatment needs separate review. Calling an account a “special needs trust” is not enough. Before changing a beneficiary designation or leaving an inheritance, have a qualified adviser check the proposed structure and payment rules. SSA’s trust guidance explains why the details matter.

ABLE example: An eligible person may use an ABLE account for qualified disability expenses. From January 1, 2026, the qualifying disability must have begun before age 46, rather than before age 26. The first $100,000 receives an SSI resource exclusion; contribution limits, withdrawal rules, and possible Medicaid claims still require attention. An ABLE account and a trust serve different functions. Neither supplies a caregiver. Read SSA’s ABLE overview.

Give the future coordinator a practical route to the money: whom to contact, what expenses may be paid, what documentation is required, and who can authorize urgent spending. Keep account credentials in an appropriately protected system rather than circulating them in a general care packet.

Investigate services before choosing a move date

Ask what stage each service has reached: initial inquiry, application, eligibility assessment, interest list, funding approval, provider search, or confirmed start. Treat these as separate milestones.

Texas service example: Texas HHS explains that demand for Home and Community-based Services exceeds available funding, so people may be placed on an interest list. The local intellectual and developmental disability authority is an entry point. Being listed does not mean care starts on a chosen date. Ask about current requirements, other available supports, and how to keep contact information current. See Texas HHS’s IDD long-term-care overview.

When school is part of the current support system, also use the years before special education ends to plan the replacement of actual support hours, transportation, and relationships.

Choose housing by the care it can sustain

A family home, supported apartment, small group home, or residential community can each work well or poorly depending on the person, staffing, safeguards, and funding. The label does not answer whether someone will receive help at 2 a.m.

Ask for a walk-through of an ordinary week, overnight coverage, illness procedures, communication support, transport, family contact, and what happens if needs increase. Ask who has authority to end the arrangement and how concerns are independently reviewed. Compare the written agreement with what you are told on a tour.

A gradual transition may protect familiarity and reveal gaps early, but it is not always immediately available or affordable. Record the ideal arrangement and the workable interim arrangement separately.

A plan for Paraguay needs its own legal and financial foundation

The U.S. examples above explain questions to investigate; they are not benefits available everywhere. SSI generally cannot continue during an entire calendar month outside the United States, with additional absence rules and limited exceptions. Do not build a Paraguay budget on the assumption that SSI follows the person. See 20 CFR §416.215 and confirm individual circumstances with SSA.

Families considering cross-border care should obtain advice on lawful residence, recognition of legal authority, health coverage, taxation, inheritance, fund transfers, and emergency travel in every relevant jurisdiction. A U.S. document or service approval should not be assumed to establish authority or funding in Paraguay.

What You Can Do This Week

  1. Write one ordinary day. Include every prompt, task, supervision period, and enjoyable activity.
  2. Name an emergency contact and a backup. Ask what each can do, how quickly they can arrive, and what they need to learn.
  3. Make a document index. List existing legal, medical, benefits, and financial records, their dates, and where authorized people can find them.
  4. Make one targeted call. Ask an agency about entry requirements or a lawyer about a specific authority or inheritance question.
  5. Set a review date. Assign the next action to a named person and review it within a month.

Long-Term Questions to Consider

  • What will change if both the disabled person and their caregivers need more support?
  • Who will notice poor care when the primary parent is gone?
  • Which funding is confirmed, and which is still an assumption?
  • How will relationships and preferences survive a move?
  • Who keeps the plan current after its original author can no longer maintain it?

Casa de SAM’s position: continuity must be built

For my family, this question is personal. Sam’s future cannot depend indefinitely on everything remaining in my head and every responsibility remaining in my hands.

Casa de SAM’s developing vision puts lifelong belonging alongside staffing, legal clarity, financial sustainability, and knowledge of each resident. Those are commitments we are working to design responsibly, not services we can offer today. Families need a present plan while future possibilities are being built.

A useful lifelong plan is a set of arrangements other people can understand, access, and carry forward. It grows stronger each time one assumption becomes a confirmed responsibility.

About Casa de SAM

Casa de SAM is a developing vision for a lifelong residential community in Paraguay for adults with autism and other developmental disabilities who need substantial daily support. The project is being designed around safety, flexible daily rhythms, meaningful choice, relationships, and belonging that does not have to be earned through productivity or independence.

Casa de SAM is not yet an operating nonprofit or residential provider. We are currently researching, documenting the vision, and looking for people who may eventually help build it responsibly.

Information reviewed October 16, 2026. This article provides general educational information and describes Casa de SAM’s developing philosophy. Laws, eligibility rules, and services vary by country and jurisdiction. This is not individualized medical, therapeutic, educational, legal, or program-placement advice.
