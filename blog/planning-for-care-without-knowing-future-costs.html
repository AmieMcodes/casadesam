Planning for Care Without Knowing Exactly What the Future Will Cost

You do not need a perfect forecast to begin. You need clear priorities, adaptable scenarios, and people who can carry the plan forward.

How can parents financially prepare for a disabled child's lifelong care when they cannot predict what that care will cost? The answer is to build a plan that can change. We do not know every future caregiver, expense, law, or medical need. Waiting until we do would mean waiting forever.

There is an uncomfortable tension in this work. Families are asked to make very long-term promises with incomplete information. We are supposed to think decades ahead while also getting dinner on the table, arranging transportation, and helping our children through the day in front of us. A useful plan has to make room for both realities.

The future matters, but so does the life we are living now

The photograph above is of Sam and me in Itauguá, Paraguay. We were on one of our many little adventures, smiling together with beautiful ñandutí in the background. I cannot tell you what his support will cost in 2046. I cannot tell you who will be on his care team then, or which housing arrangements will be available.

What I can say is that Samuel deserves to enjoy today. He should have more photographs like this one: adventures, ordinary afternoons, small discoveries, and the comfort of knowing his life is worth celebrating. Planning for his future is not a reason to put his present life on hold. It is how I try to protect the possibility of many more good days.

For Casa de SAM, that distinction is foundational. We need numbers, safeguards, and professional advice. But the numbers are in service of a person, not the other way around.

Begin with a range of scenarios instead of one prediction

A single “lifetime cost” figure is emotionally satisfying because it looks definitive. Unfortunately, it is usually built on assumptions about care hours, health, housing, geography, and future inflation that no family can guarantee. A more useful starting point is to outline three scenarios.

  • Expected support: Based on assistance the person needs today, with reasonable changes as they age. Include housing, daily staff, transportation, health care, activities, and administration.
  • Higher-support years: What if the person requires greater supervision, more frequent medical care, accessible equipment, or additional one-to-one help?
  • Disruption or crisis: What if the primary caregiver dies, an arrangement closes, a support worker leaves suddenly, or a temporary emergency requires a different home?

For each scenario, identify what you know, what you are estimating, what would trigger a change, and who would be authorized to respond. Update those assumptions rather than treating your first spreadsheet as a promise about the future.

Our companion article, How Much Does Lifelong Care for an Autistic Adult Cost?, explains the expenses that should be included before families begin comparing numbers.

Inflation makes a fixed number less useful over time

Even if a family's current estimate were exactly right, the price of services would not necessarily stay fixed. To illustrate the math, if a service costs $1,000 a month today and rises by an assumed 3% per year for 20 years, the comparable nominal monthly cost would be about $1,806. That is simply $1,000 multiplied by 1.03 to the twentieth power. It is an example, not a forecast.

Actual increases may be faster or slower, and wages, medications, housing, exchange rates, and specialist services do not all change at the same pace. A long-term plan should therefore include inflation ranges and periodic reviews, not just a dollar target written once and forgotten.

For a family earning in one currency and spending in another, as cross-border families sometimes do, currency risk adds another uncertainty. That is a reason to ask for qualified financial guidance and to label assumptions clearly; it is not a reason to abandon the plan.

A financial plan and a legal plan do different jobs

Having savings does not automatically give a future caregiver authority to manage them. Naming someone in a document does not guarantee that public benefits, housing, or health services will be available. The person who helps with everyday decisions may not be the same person authorized to make particular legal or financial decisions.

  1. Resources: What assets, benefits, insurance, charitable programs, or other support may be available, and under what conditions?
  2. Authority: Who may legally access money, arrange services, consent where required, or advocate for the adult? Which less restrictive decision-making tools should be considered?
  3. Instructions and preferences: What does the person enjoy, refuse, fear, or need? How do they communicate assent, discomfort, and changes in routine?
  4. Accountability: Who checks that funds and authority are actually being used for the intended person's wellbeing?

The Administration for Community Living emphasizes planning led by the person's goals and preferences. The same agency's supported decision-making resources are a reminder that needing help does not automatically remove an adult's voice.

Understand benefits tools without assuming they travel across borders

In the United States, families may discuss disability benefits, special-needs trusts, and ABLE accounts with qualified advisers. In 2026, ABLE eligibility can include qualifying disabilities whose onset was before age 46, and the standard annual contribution limit is $20,000, subject to applicable rules and possible additional contributions for some working beneficiaries. The Social Security Administration's ABLE guidance and the IRS 2026 adjustments explain the federal framework.

That does not mean every family qualifies, or that an ABLE account solves the whole problem. State program details and public-benefit consequences matter. A family in Paraguay cannot simply assume American benefit rules apply locally, that payments are portable, or that a U.S. legal structure substitutes for Paraguayan requirements. The appropriate professional may need expertise in both jurisdictions.

Build a plan that someone else can understand

A thick binder that only its creator can navigate is not enough. Imagine a trusted person receiving a call tonight and needing to help your child tomorrow. What would that person need first?

  • An emergency contact list with a primary and backup decision-maker.
  • A plain-language routine: food, communication, medication instructions from treating clinicians, sensory needs, travel safety, sleep, and favorite activities.
  • A current inventory of services, benefits, accounts, housing arrangements, important documents, and renewal dates.
  • A list of advisers and an explanation of what legal authority each person actually has.
  • A plan for reviewing finances and care at least annually, and sooner after a major change.

A letter of intent can help future supporters understand the person, but it is generally not a substitute for enforceable legal documents. A local attorney should explain which arrangements are appropriate and recognized where the person lives.

What You Can Do This Week

  1. Write down the care you currently provide without pay. Estimate what would need to be replaced if you were unavailable.
  2. Create three budget columns—expected, higher-support, and crisis—and mark each line as known, estimated, or unknown.
  3. Choose an inflation assumption for illustrations, then test a higher and a lower one to see what changes.
  4. Ask a qualified local professional about benefits, decision-making authority, estate planning, and cross-border complications.
  5. Give a trusted backup person a short, current “what Sam needs today” guide, and schedule a date to review it.

For a simpler first step, read How to Begin Future Planning Without Having Every Answer. For a broader structure, see A Parent's Guide to Planning for Lifelong Disability Care.

Long-Term Questions to Consider

What happens when the first backup caregiver is no longer available? How will the adult's preferences be heard by someone new? What financial conditions would require a different housing arrangement? Are there independent people empowered to ask hard questions? And how will the plan change when our children change?

Casa de SAM's Position

Casa de SAM is still a developing vision for lifelong housing and support in Paraguay, not a guarantee of a placement or an operating care service. I want its design to acknowledge uncertainty honestly: individualized plans, prudent finances, strong oversight, and room for lives that are not confined to a budget sheet.

I cannot promise to predict Samuel's future. I can keep learning, keep making practical preparations, and keep building relationships that may one day help carry the responsibility. In the meantime, we will go on our adventures. A responsible plan for tomorrow should help us treasure today, not steal it.

About Casa de SAM

Casa de SAM is a developing vision for a lifelong residential community in Paraguay for adults with developmental disabilities who need substantial daily support. Its founding principles center safety, dignity, joyful daily life, and lasting belonging after full acceptance.

Casa de SAM is not yet an operating nonprofit or residential provider. We are researching and developing the legal, financial, staffing, and oversight structures needed to build the vision responsibly.
