The Questions Every Parent Should Ask About Their Disabled Child’s Future

The most useful question is often the follow-up: who will do it, under what authority, with what resources, and what happens if they cannot?

What should you ask about your disabled child’s future when the real concern is much bigger than one program or one place to live?

Ask questions that reveal how care will work in practice. “Will someone look after him?” is a beginning. “Who notices when he is in pain, how do they recognize it, and who checks that they respond?” gets closer to an answer another person can act on.

The questions below are a conversation guide for relatives, service coordinators, providers, and qualified legal or financial advisers. They are not a test every family must finish at once. If you need the overall structure first, use our guide to planning for lifelong disability care.

1. What does my child want, and how will others know?

Start with the person whose future is being discussed. Ask how they show enjoyment, hesitation, refusal, discomfort, and preference. Can they answer verbally, point, use pictures, use a device, choose between real objects, or participate through a familiar routine?

For someone with limited conventional communication, distinguish observation from interpretation. “She repeatedly moves away from this room when the machine starts” is an observation. “She hates all group activities” is a much larger conclusion that may be wrong.

Follow up: Who will teach unfamiliar caregivers the communication system? What happens if a device is unavailable? How will staff check a first interpretation instead of allowing it to become a permanent label?

Look for: concrete examples, accessible choices, and a willingness to revise assumptions. Be cautious when every answer comes from adults talking about convenience rather than the person’s experience.

The candy in this photograph came from a neighbor here in Paraguay. We did not know her well beyond greeting each other on the street, but when we walked past she excitedly called us over with a large bag she had saved for Sam. Neighbors sometimes offer fruit from their trees or set aside a party bag for him. Those everyday connections matter when I think about his future. Alongside asking who will provide care, I want to ask who will know him, remember him, and be glad to see him.

What family research adds to these conversations

Chung Eun Lee, Meghan Burke, and Claire Stelter interviewed ten parent-sibling pairs for a 2019 study. Both generations described family and system barriers; siblings specifically wanted more communication about planning. Twenty interviewees cannot represent every family, and the findings do not make siblings default caregivers. They support a practical caution: do not mistake unspoken expectations for an agreement. Read the study abstract.

2. What happens if I am unavailable tomorrow?

Ask who can provide immediate safe care, who has agreed to a longer stay, and which tasks exceed their experience. The person closest geographically may not be able to handle the whole routine.

Try a simple scenario: the primary caregiver goes to the hospital and cannot use their phone. Who starts the backup plan? How does someone get into the home? Who knows the medication list, food routine, communication, and required supervision? Where is lawful decision-making authority recorded?

Follow up: Has the backup caregiver spent time doing the routine, or have they only said they would help? When can you practice a manageable part together?

Do not force a risky trial to prove a point. Start with a familiar activity while established support remains available. The goal is to discover missing knowledge early.

3. What will an ordinary week contain?

Ask to map mornings, afternoons, evenings, weekends, and transport. A program brochure may describe activities without explaining the hours before it opens, the days it closes, or what happens when the person needs to stay home.

Imagine a day service offering 20 hours each week. That leaves most of the 168-hour week outside the program. Not every remaining hour needs paid staffing, but every hour that requires supervision or hands-on support needs a realistic arrangement.

Follow up: Who provides personal care during activities? Is transportation included? Can the person decline an activity? What happens when they are tired, ill, or overwhelmed? Which valued relationships continue?

As adulthood approaches, ask these questions before the school schedule disappears. Turning 18 can introduce separate legal and service changes; a birthday should not be the first time the family investigates them.

4. What does “24-hour support” mean here?

Ask who is physically present overnight, whether they are awake or asleep, how many people they support, and what happens if two people need help simultaneously. Ask whether a manager being available by phone is being described as overnight care.

Explain the actual support need rather than requesting a reassuring label: help after waking, supervision around exits, assistance with transfers, or a particular care procedure. Ask the provider to explain its safe capacity and limits.

Follow up: Who covers an absent worker? What training is completed before someone works alone? When is outside medical assistance sought? Who remains with the other residents if someone needs hospital care?

A credible answer can include a limitation. A provider that cannot safely meet a particular need should say so before placement.

5. What would make this home remain a home?

Ask about personal space, privacy, visitors, possessions, food, daily schedules, community access, and the ability to raise a concern. Ask what happens if care becomes harder or funding changes. Read the discharge, termination, fee-change, and appeal provisions before relying on a promise of permanence.

U.S. federal example: The Medicaid rule at 42 CFR §441.301(c)(4) addresses community access, dignity, privacy, and choice in applicable home- and community-based settings. Provider-controlled residences have additional requirements, including enforceable residency protections and choice of visitors. Some modifications require an individualized assessed need and documented safeguards. These are specific Medicaid requirements, not universal rules for every home worldwide. Read §441.301(c)(4).

Follow up: If a restriction is proposed, what problem is it addressing, what alternatives have been tried, and when will it be reviewed? Can someone outside the daily care team help the resident question it?

Compare actual arrangements as well as principles. Our overview of where an autistic adult might live can help organize the options.

6. Who has authority, and who checks their work?

Ask which decisions the adult can make, which require help, and which may need lawful representation. Separate medical decisions, access to information, financial transactions, benefit administration, and personal choices. Ask a qualified local adviser to identify the scope and limits of each document.

Texas’s supported decision-making statute, for example, says the supporter assists rather than deciding for the adult. That distinction is worth taking to a lawyer when comparing options; it does not establish what is appropriate for someone who cannot make a particular decision. See Texas Estates Code §1357.051.

Follow up: If one person controls money and care arrangements, who independently hears the disabled person’s concerns? What happens if relatives disagree? Who can challenge a decision, review spending, or request a different arrangement?

Safeguards should address the possibility that a well-intentioned person becomes exhausted or unavailable as well as the possibility of misuse.

7. What money is actually available for this arrangement?

Separate confirmed income and approved services from expected inheritances, hoped-for fundraising, applications in progress, and unpaid family help. Ask what each funding source may cover, who controls it, and what must be reported.

If a U.S. family is considering an ABLE account, ask who has signature authority, how expenses will be recorded, and how withdrawals interact with benefits. SSA explains that an authorized person can administer an account for someone unable to manage it. That account authority does not appoint a daily caregiver or create general medical decision-making authority. See SSA’s ABLE guidance.

Follow up: What happens if a parent’s income disappears, a provider raises fees, a vehicle needs replacement, or extra staffing is required? Which costs are excluded from a quoted rate?

For international arrangements, ask agencies and advisers to confirm portability, residence requirements, and local coverage. A familiar U.S. benefit name is not proof that it can fund care in Paraguay.

8. Who will notice that life has become smaller?

Care can deteriorate without a dramatic incident. A person may stop seeing friends, rarely leave home, lose access to a preferred activity, or become less willing to approach a caregiver. Families should decide who will maintain enough contact to notice those changes.

Ask how concerns can be raised privately and accessibly, how health or distress changes are reviewed, and whether someone independent of the provider stays involved. For a nonspeaking adult, familiar people may help interpret changes, but familiarity should never substitute for appropriate assessment when something seems wrong.

Follow up: What does a good month look like for this person? Which signs would prompt a conversation? Who makes sure that conversation results in action?

Turn answers into a record you can use

After a conversation, write five things: the question, the answer, what confirms it, the unresolved point, and the person responsible for the next step. “Transport may be available” remains unresolved. “The coordinator will confirm the route and support level by Friday” creates a task.

A missing answer is useful information. Record it without treating the whole plan as a failure. Prioritize gaps that threaten immediate safety or create an approaching deadline.

What You Can Do This Week

  1. Choose three questions. Pick the ones with the largest consequences for your family now.
  2. Arrange one conversation. Send the questions in advance to a provider, relative, coordinator, or adviser.
  3. Ask for one concrete example. Use a situation involving overnight care, transport, a missed shift, or communication.
  4. Confirm the answer. Request the relevant policy, written service details, or official source rather than relying on a general promise.
  5. Assign the next step. Record a person and date, including when you will follow up if no answer arrives.

Long-Term Questions to Consider

  • Will the person’s communication and preferences remain central as supporters change?
  • Which responsibilities depend entirely on one family member or employee?
  • How will you compare a proposed arrangement with the person’s actual experience of it?
  • What happens if health needs or care costs increase?
  • Who will keep asking these questions after you cannot?

Casa de SAM’s position: serious promises welcome specific questions

These are questions Casa de SAM must work toward answering, too. A future residential community should be examined through its governance, staffing, funding, safeguards, and treatment of individual residents—not only the warmth of its vision.

Our project is still in planning. We cannot offer families a current placement or guarantee a future admission. We can be clear about the standard we want to build toward: a person should remain known, supported, and able to enjoy a life that belongs to them.

About Casa de SAM

Casa de SAM is a developing vision for a lifelong residential community in Paraguay for adults with autism and other developmental disabilities who need substantial daily support. The project is being designed around safety, flexible daily rhythms, meaningful choice, relationships, and belonging that does not have to be earned through productivity or independence.

Casa de SAM is not yet an operating nonprofit or residential provider. We are currently researching, documenting the vision, and looking for people who may eventually help build it responsibly.
