PRACTICAL STEPS & FUTURE PLANNING · SEPTEMBER 25, 2026

How to Begin Future Planning Without Having Every Answer

Start with what another person would need to know this week. The permanent housing decision can remain open while the practical plan gets stronger.

What if you cannot choose a future home, cannot predict your child’s adult needs, and do not yet know who could take over your responsibilities?

You can still begin. A future-care plan can start as one page of reliable information, one confirmed backup arrangement, and a short list of unanswered questions with next steps attached. It does not have to begin with a permanent placement decision.

Uncertainty becomes more manageable when you distinguish what must work now from what can be investigated over time. The purpose of this first month is to reduce dependence on one person’s memory—not to solve the rest of your child’s life in thirty days.

For the bigger picture, our parent’s guide to lifelong disability care explains how daily support, legal authority, funding, housing, and oversight fit together.

On the evening pictured here, we were in Ñemby when Sam stopped at a shop and picked out his own toys and little games. It was simply a fun evening of his making choices. Recording moments like that helps a future caregiver see more than the assistance he needs: it shows interests to make room for and choices to keep offering. He does not need to choose every part of a future care arrangement to have a meaningful say in his day.

Support can help families move from worry to action

In a 2006 randomized study, Tamar Heller and Joe Caldwell evaluated a peer-support intervention for future planning, following 29 intervention families and 19 control families for a year. The intervention helped families take concrete steps, including completing letters of intent and acting on residential planning. This older study does not validate the thirty-day sequence below or guarantee outcomes in Paraguay. It offers evidence that planning support can produce practical action. Read the study abstract.

The first-month sequence here is our suggested way to break the work into manageable pieces, not a tested treatment or a deadline every family must meet.

Decide what cannot wait

Begin with two questions. If you became unavailable tonight, which support would be missing? Is there a deadline approaching that could affect access to services, documents, or a transition?

Those questions determine the first action. If there is no backup caregiver, start with a conversation about immediate care. If an application deadline is close, confirm the requirements. If essential information exists only in your memory, write the first page.

Give everything else a place on the list. “Investigate future housing” is legitimate unfinished work. It does not need to block “show a trusted person how this evening routine works.”

Week one: write a minimum useful support profile

Use an ordinary routine as your starting point: breakfast, getting ready to leave home, a familiar outing, or bedtime. Describe what happens in order and what the person does independently.

Then add the support that makes the routine work. Does someone prepare items in advance? Offer a choice? Stay close to an exit? Notice when waiting has become too much? Supply help that an observer might miss?

For a first page, include:

  • How the person communicates yes, no, help, discomfort, and finished.
  • What they enjoy and what usually makes the routine easier.
  • The supervision or assistance needed during that routine.
  • Important health and safety information, with the location of current professional instructions.
  • Whom an authorized caregiver should contact when something changes.

Write observable detail. “Likes things a certain way” gives another person little to use. “Offer the two familiar cups and allow time for a choice before pouring” describes an action. Distinguish essential safety directions from preferences and conveniences.

A support profile is not a treatment plan or a legal authorization. Keep medical instructions current with the treating professional, and ask the relevant professional about any procedure a substitute caregiver would need to perform.

Week two: make one piece of the routine transferable

Choose a trusted adult who is willing to learn a manageable role. Ask what they can actually provide rather than asking whether they could “take care of everything.”

For example: “Could you learn our usual evening routine so you could help for two hours if I had an appointment?” creates a specific conversation about availability, comfort, training, and limits.

Review the written profile together, then share a familiar activity with established support present. Notice which directions were unclear, which supplies were hard to find, and which choices required more time than you had written down. Ask the disabled person for feedback in the ways available to them.

Do not interpret distress as proof that future support is impossible. It may show that a person needs more familiarity, a different helper, clearer communication, or a slower approach. Equally, do not override repeated refusal merely to complete a planning exercise.

The result may be a better page and a narrower agreement: this person can help with an outing, but cannot safely provide overnight care. That is useful progress.

Week three: verify one system instead of researching every system

Choose the unanswered question with the highest practical consequence. It might concern adult services, decision-making authority, benefits, a school transition, or the handling of an inheritance.

Prepare a short call note: the question, the person’s relevant circumstances, the office contacted, the response, the official source or written confirmation, and the next action. Keep sensitive records private and share them only through appropriate channels with authorized recipients.

Service-access example: In Texas, local intellectual and developmental disability authorities use Form 8591 to collect information for HCS and Texas Home Living interest-list entry. A specific first question is: “Which office handles my request, and how will I confirm the date it was recorded?” That is more useful than a general search for adult autism services. Texas HHS explains the form’s purpose.

Money example: If a relative wants to leave funds directly to someone receiving means-tested benefits, ask a qualified adviser how to coordinate the gift with benefits before changing documents. SSA’s trust guidance explains that legal structure and distributions affect treatment. A first appointment can identify the questions and records needed; you need not arrive with your own trust design. See SSA’s explanation of trusts and SSI.

Families in Paraguay should direct equivalent questions to the relevant Paraguayan agency and qualified local adviser. These examples show how to ask a focused question; they do not establish local eligibility or legal rights.

Week four: turn unknowns into an action list

Create four headings: confirmed, being investigated, not yet arranged, and next review. Keep a proposal out of the confirmed column until the people and resources needed have actually been agreed.

A relative’s willingness to learn is confirmed willingness. It is not yet confirmed overnight coverage. An appointment with a lawyer is a scheduled consultation. It is not a completed legal arrangement. An application receipt proves submission. It does not prove funding or a start date.

This is not an exercise in finding fault with your progress. It prevents a hopeful conversation from being mistaken for a functioning safeguard.

For every priority gap, name one next action, one responsible person, and a date. Keep the active list short enough that it can survive an ordinary week of caregiving.

Use principles while the permanent choices remain open

You may not know the address of a future home. You can still record what a suitable home must support: communication, safe movement, personal care, relationships, quiet space, privacy, and activities the person values.

For applicable U.S. Medicaid HCBS waivers, 42 CFR §441.301(c)(1)–(3) describes a person-centered process that includes chosen supporters, accessible participation, strengths and preferences, support needs, risk measures, and backup strategies. The service plan must be reviewed at least annually, when needs change significantly, or at the individual’s request. This is a specific legal framework, not a requirement imposed on every private family plan. Read the planning provisions.

A family can use a similar practical habit without pretending to have completed a formal service plan: document the person, test whether information is usable, assign responsibilities, and return to the plan as life changes.

When you are ready for the next conversation, use the questions every parent should ask about their disabled child’s future. Choose the few that matter most now.

A letter of intent can preserve what forms miss

Alongside official records, write a personal care letter. Explain the person’s ordinary rhythms, important relationships, preferred ways of receiving help, and what you hope future caregivers will understand.

Include enjoyable things. A file containing only diagnoses, risks, and problems can leave a successor with instructions for preventing harm but little understanding of how to share a good day.

Keep the letter practical and dated. State which details are current, where formal records are stored, and whom to ask when something is unclear. Have an appropriate professional prepare legal documents separately. The letter carries knowledge; it does not independently appoint a guardian, authorize treatment, or change an inheritance.

What You Can Do This Week

  1. Reserve one short planning session. Choose a realistic twenty-minute block and one task.
  2. Write one routine. Include independence, prompts, direct help, preferences, and the support an observer might miss.
  3. Ask one person about a specific role. Record what they can and cannot agree to.
  4. Prepare one agency or adviser question. Write it before making the call and keep the answer with the source and date.
  5. Schedule the next review. Choose the next task now, while the current one is still fresh.

Long-Term Questions to Consider

  • Which ordinary routines could become less dependent on one caregiver?
  • What deadlines will matter over the next year?
  • Who can help maintain records and follow up on unfinished tasks?
  • What support would become necessary if the caregiver’s health changed?
  • Which future choices should remain flexible as the person’s own preferences develop?

Casa de SAM’s position: begin before certainty arrives

Casa de SAM began with a question that could not be answered in one conversation: what kind of lifelong home could remain when a parent no longer could?

The vision is still being developed. It does not remove my responsibility to plan for Sam now, and it should not replace another family’s emergency arrangements or current service applications.

Beginning earlier gives a family time to learn, confirm, and revise. One useful page, one willing person, and one completed follow-up will not finish the whole future. They will make the next step possible.

About Casa de SAM

Casa de SAM is a developing vision for a lifelong residential community in Paraguay for adults with autism and other developmental disabilities who need substantial daily support. The project is being designed around safety, flexible daily rhythms, meaningful choice, relationships, and belonging that does not have to be earned through productivity or independence.

Casa de SAM is not yet an operating nonprofit or residential provider. We are currently researching, documenting the vision, and looking for people who may eventually help build it responsibly.
