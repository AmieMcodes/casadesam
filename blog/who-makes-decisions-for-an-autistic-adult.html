LEGAL RIGHTS AND ADULT DECISION-MAKING · OCTOBER 9, 2026

Who Makes Decisions for an Autistic Adult Who Cannot Live Independently?

A practical responsibility map for health care, money, housing, daily choices, emergencies, and long-term protection.

A son or daughter may need help dressing, eating, communicating, crossing a street, or understanding a complicated medical choice long after childhood. That is a question about support. It does not, by itself, answer a different question: who has legal authority to make each adult decision?

For parents, the transition can be unsettling. Years of scheduling every appointment and handling every form can make it feel natural to continue. Yet adulthood changes the legal picture, and there may be no single document that makes one parent "in charge" of everything. The correct arrangement depends on the adult, the decision, the jurisdiction, and any valid authorization or court process.

There is also a human truth that legal forms cannot capture: decisions still concern a person with preferences and relationships. Can an Autistic Adult Live Independently? separates the ability to live alone from the much wider question of living a meaningful adult life with appropriate support.

The question is not "Who controls this person?"

A more useful starting point is to divide the problem into five areas: health care, money, housing and services, everyday choices, and emergencies. In each area, ask who can explain the decision, who can make or communicate it, who has legally recognized authority if needed, and who will carry out the result.

Some autistic adults make decisions independently when information is presented accessibly. Others make certain choices with trusted support and need more formal arrangements for some complex transactions. Some situations require an authorized representative. A person's ability and legal rights cannot be inferred from a diagnosis, a speech assessment alone, or whether they can live without help.

The U.S. Administration for Community Living describes supported decision-making as individualized assistance that keeps the adult at the center of the decision. It can involve a team and can vary by decision type.

Health care: distinguish support, consent, and treatment

Picture an adult who is non-speaking, frightened of unfamiliar clinics, and needs a tooth extracted. A trusted caregiver can explain the visit using pictures, help the dentist understand pain signals, and remain with the patient. That practical support is essential. But it is separate from the legal question of who may authorize a procedure when formal consent is required.

The treating team should assess and support the adult's ability to participate in the particular decision using appropriate communication methods. If a lawful surrogate or other authorization is needed, the clinic should identify what the applicable law recognizes. A parent-child relationship, a residential placement, or a benefits appointment does not automatically answer that question.

In emergencies, clinicians may have authority to provide urgent care under rules different from routine treatment. Families should not assume that a rule used for a life-threatening emergency governs ordinary medication changes, elective procedures, or daily personal care.

For non-urgent distress or refusal, first ask whether pain, sensory overload, communication barriers, or prior trauma are part of the response. A safe care plan makes room for those answers.

Money: a helper, an agent, and a payee are different roles

Imagine the same adult receives public benefits, earns a small amount from an activity, and has money saved by relatives. Who pays the bills? Who reviews the statements? Who is allowed to sign at a bank?

In the United States, the Social Security Administration can appoint a representative payee to manage Social Security or SSI benefits for someone who needs that help. The SSA's official guide makes an important limitation clear: payee authority concerns those benefits, not unrelated income, property, or medical decisions.

A valid financial power of attorney, trust, conservatorship, guardianship of the estate, or other recognized arrangement may address different assets, depending on local law and the person's ability to execute documents. They are not interchangeable. A supporter helping someone compare banking choices may have no authority to sign a contract on that person's behalf.

A strong plan names both the authorized person and a second person who can review records or raise concerns. Money should never become an unexamined extension of a family's household finances.

Housing: the provider is not automatically the legal decision-maker

A residential provider may help with medication schedules, transport, daily routines, and emergencies. It may know the adult extraordinarily well. None of that by itself makes the provider the legal owner of the person's choices.

Before any move, ask who can authorize the agreement, what the resident can choose about roommates and visitors, who receives incident reports, who pays, who can complain, and how the person can leave or challenge a change of placement. The answers should not live only in a contract understood by the parent.

In U.S. home- and community-based Medicaid settings covered by federal requirements, 42 CFR 441.301 includes person-centered planning and rights-related requirements. That regulation is an example of a specific U.S. program framework, not a rule governing all group homes or any future residence in Paraguay.

Everyday decisions: make space where the consequences are ordinary

What time should the adult wake up? Which clothes feel comfortable? Who do they want to see? Do they want a quiet evening or a walk? What happens when they say no to a preferred staff activity?

It is possible to provide nearly complete physical support and still organize the day around the person's expressed choices. Supports may include two tangible options, a predictable routine, an AAC system, allowing extra time, or recognizing consistent patterns without assuming they are infallible.

A communication profile can identify reliable signals and their limits: "When offered two objects, she consistently reaches for the one she wants"; "He sometimes walks away when overwhelmed, which should not be treated automatically as a considered refusal of medical treatment." Specific observations help a new caregiver listen without pretending to know more than the evidence shows.

That circle can include friends, not only family members or professionals. In the photograph with this article, Sam is standing near a game-and-prize counter with our friend Monica just behind him. People like Monica can help him explore choices during play, notice what interests him, and bring their own caring perspective to conversations about his well-being. Her observations matter. That does not make her a legal decision-maker or give her the right to override what Sam is communicating; it means we should value the wider community that helps him be heard.

What changes at adulthood in the United States?

For example, the Texas Governor's disability guidance explains that students generally gain their own educational decision-making rights at 18, subject to relevant legal arrangements. Texas law requires serious attention to supports and less restrictive alternatives before a court imposes guardianship. The Texas supported decision-making statute also lets qualifying adults voluntarily select supporters without surrendering their own decision authority.

Those are Texas-specific examples, not a universal U.S. rule and not directions for a Paraguayan family. Families should check age-of-majority rules, privacy law, health-consent law, education rights, and court procedures in the jurisdiction that actually governs their situation.

What is different in Paraguay?

Paraguay adopted a major change through Law No. 7371/2025. The country's Judicial Branch says the law took effect July 16, 2025, replacing older disability-related interdiction and curatorship mechanisms with individualized supports intended to respect the person's will and preferences.

Because Casa de SAM is planned for Paraguay, we must not write policies as though an American guardianship model automatically applies. Questions about selecting supporters, documenting legal powers, responding to disputes, and validating foreign documents need current Paraguayan legal guidance. The Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, which Paraguay has ratified, provides an important rights framework, but local legal procedure matters.

Make one responsibility map before choosing documents

A simple worksheet is often more useful than an impressive folder of unsigned forms. Create these columns for each decision:

  • Decision: for example, consenting to a non-emergency surgery, paying rent, selecting a daily activity, or changing a residence.
  • The person's participation: what communication and accessible information will be offered; what preferences are already known; what is still uncertain.
  • Supporter: the person who explains options, helps communicate, or coordinates a discussion.
  • Legal authority: the adult themselves or a specifically documented representative, if the law requires one. Record which law or document you verified.
  • Implementer: the clinician, caregiver, banker, or service provider who carries out the decision.
  • Safeguard and backup: who checks for conflict of interest, hears complaints, and steps in when the first person is unavailable.

A filled example might read: "Routine dental visit / adult uses pictures and gestures / aunt accompanies and helps communicate / clinic verifies consent requirements and any authorized representative / dentist provides care / independent advocate receives unresolved concerns." The family does not need to guess a legal answer to finish the planning exercise. A blank legal-authority box is a question to take to counsel.

What You Can Do This Week

  • Pick one upcoming medical or financial decision and fill in the six fields above, marking unknown legal authority clearly.
  • Ask the provider which documents it will require and whether it can accommodate the adult's communication and support person.
  • Create a one-page contact sheet separating practical caregivers from people with verified legal authority.
  • Identify a backup for any role currently held by only one aging parent or family member.
  • Schedule a jurisdiction-specific conversation with a disability-rights advocate or qualified attorney before signing anything that removes rights.

Long-Term Questions to Consider

If the parent dies, who can act immediately, and who can provide the longer-term care? If the adult moves between countries, which permissions need review? Who can appeal an unsuitable placement or challenge a supporter? What happens when a person communicates a new preference that conflicts with an old care plan? How will staff learn the difference between routine choice and high-stakes consent?

Casa de SAM's Position

A future resident may need someone close every hour and still deserve a voice in every decision where their preferences can be understood and respected. Casa de SAM's vision separates the practical work of caregiving from lawful decision-making authority and requires staff to learn each resident's communication over time.

As we develop a lifelong residential community in Paraguay, our responsibility is to create clear procedures for support, consent, documentation, independent oversight, and continuity after families can no longer carry the burden. We are not an operating facility yet. These are standards the project must build toward, not services we can claim to be providing today.

About Casa de SAM

Casa de SAM is a developing vision for a lifelong residential community in Paraguay for adults with developmental disabilities who need substantial daily support. Its founding principles center safety, dignity, joyful daily life, and lasting belonging after full acceptance.

Casa de SAM is not yet an operating nonprofit or residential provider. We are researching and developing the legal, financial, staffing, and oversight structures needed to build the vision responsibly.
