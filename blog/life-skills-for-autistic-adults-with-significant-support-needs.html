Life Skills for Autistic Adults With Significant Support Needs

Life skills should make daily life safer, easier, more comfortable, or more enjoyable—not quietly become a test of whether someone can live without support.

When families hear the phrase life skills, it can sound like a checklist for independence.

Can he dress himself? Cook a meal? Wash his hair? Use money? Take transportation alone? Keep a house clean?

Those are certainly life skills. But for autistic adults with significant support needs, measuring success only by whether a person can perform a task independently can miss something important.

A life skill can also be learning to participate in a routine.

It can mean choosing between two shirts while someone else helps with dressing. It can mean placing clothes in the hamper even if someone else runs the washing machine. It can mean tolerating part of a dental routine, carrying an AAC device to an appointment, learning a safe waiting place at a bus stop, or communicating that something hurts.

Sometimes the most useful skill is not eliminating support. It is making support work better.

Daily living skills can keep developing

Research on autism has repeatedly found that adaptive daily living abilities can look very different from a person's cognitive abilities. In a longitudinal study following participants to age 21, Vanessa Hus Bal and colleagues found continued development of daily living skills through childhood and adolescence, but substantial differences in eventual skill levels between autistic individuals.

Another longitudinal study of 397 autistic adolescents and adults found that daily living skills generally improved through adolescence and the early twenties before leveling off later in adulthood. Intellectual disability was associated with lower initial skill levels and slower growth, but development was not simply finished when childhood ended.

Skills are worth supporting. Lifelong support needs are not evidence that the person—or the family—failed.

What counts as a life skill?

Life skills can include personal hygiene, dressing, eating, meal preparation, cleaning, laundry, medication routines, communication, money use, transportation, community safety, shopping, leisure, and knowing how to seek help.

For someone with significant support needs, each of those areas may contain many smaller skills.

“Taking a shower” might include recognizing the bathroom routine, choosing the water temperature, stepping safely into the shower, tolerating hair washing, participating in drying, choosing clothes afterward, or communicating discomfort.

“Going shopping” might include choosing an item, carrying it, waiting in line, tolerating the checkout process, understanding that the item becomes theirs after payment, and keeping track of it on the way home.

There is no rule that says all of those steps have to belong to the same person. Support can fill the gaps.

The UK's NICE guideline for autistic adults specifically recommends structured and predictable life-skills training for autistic adults who need help with activities of daily living, across levels of intellectual ability. It also instructs professionals to recognize that adaptive functioning can differ substantially from measured intellectual ability.

Teaching useful skills is therefore completely compatible with acknowledging lifelong disability.

The question is not, “How do we make this adult need nobody?”

A better question may be, “Which abilities would make this person's own daily life safer, easier, more comfortable, or more enjoyable?”

That is also why independence should not be the only goal and why the right supported-living arrangement is often defined by the quality of support rather than by how little assistance appears on paper.

Sometimes a skill changes

One reason I resist treating life skills like permanent boxes to check is Sam's experience with haircuts.

When he was little, he loved going to children's haircut places. The photo accompanying this article was taken in Colorado Springs. He was perfectly happy in the little firetruck chair while someone used clippers around his head.

Eventually, that changed.

He developed a fear of the sound of clippers.

Today we have a very different haircut routine.

That does not make the younger haircut the “successful” one and the current routine a failure. It means his needs changed, so the support changed.

Autistic people can change across a lifetime. Sensory tolerances can change. Health can change. Communication can change. Mobility can change. Anxiety can change. Aging certainly changes people.

A lifelong support plan has to be able to change with them.

Participation can be a meaningful goal

Families sometimes face an unnecessary choice between two extremes.

At one extreme is: He must learn to do this entirely alone.

At the other is: He cannot do it alone, so there is no point teaching any part of it.

There is a large middle.

An adult may brush some teeth while a caregiver finishes the places they miss. They may put ingredients into a bowl while someone else operates the oven. They may choose groceries while another adult manages payment. They may take medication willingly when it is presented in a familiar routine without understanding the entire medical system around it.

They may hold out an arm for a blood-pressure cuff, put their feet into shoes, carry their dirty plate to the kitchen, choose the music during cleaning, or hand someone the shampoo they prefer.

Those are not fake accomplishments because assistance remains.

Support and ability can exist in the same task.

Not every possible skill is worth the same effort

There is another uncomfortable question families should be allowed to ask:

What is this skill going to cost the person to learn?

Some skills are worth enormous effort because they improve safety, health, communication, comfort, or access to the community.

Other skills may technically be teachable but require years of prompting, stress, therapy, conflict, or repetition for a result that changes very little about the person's actual quality of life.

That does not mean teaching is bad. It means goals should have a reason.

For one adult, learning to prepare breakfast may create enormous freedom.

For another, the better investment may be learning to reliably indicate hunger and choose between foods while someone else prepares the meal.

For one person, independent public transportation may be realistic and transformative.

For another, learning to sit in a safe location, stay with a support person, tolerate the ride, recognize a familiar destination, or carry identification may be much more valuable.

The right target depends on the person.

Research also suggests that support for adaptive skills should not simply disappear once school does. One longitudinal study following autistic people into their twenties found daily living skill trajectories plateaued and then declined after high-school exit, underscoring the importance of continued opportunities to use and support those abilities in adulthood.

Life skills should support a life

A person can become very good at making a bed and still be lonely.

They can learn to fold towels and still have no meaningful control over their day.

They can complete hygiene routines perfectly and never get to go anywhere they enjoy.

Life-skills work should therefore sit inside a much larger question: What kind of life are these skills helping the person live?

The point of teaching laundry is not laundry. The point might be greater participation in one's own home.

The point of shopping practice is not proving someone understands commerce. The point might be choosing favorite food, clothing, or belongings.

The point of transportation skills is not graduating from needing another human. The point might be having more of the world available.

That distinction matters.

It is also why Casa de SAM rejects the idea that residents should have to earn belonging through progress, classes, work, or independence. Skills may be offered and supported. Home is not conditional on mastering them.

Our earlier guide to meaningful daily activities for adults with intellectual disabilities makes the same broader point: a useful day is not measured only by visible productivity.

What you can do this week

Watch one ordinary routine

Choose one ordinary daily routine—not the skill your family feels most guilty about—and watch it carefully. Write down which parts the person already does, which parts they participate in with help, which parts cause distress, and which parts someone else currently handles.

Choose one useful change

Identify one small change that would make that routine more useful to the person. It might be offering a choice, moving supplies where they are easier to reach, adding a visual cue, changing the order, changing the sensory experience, or teaching one repeatable step.

Ask why the skill matters

If the answer is mostly “because adults are supposed to do this,” consider whether there is a more valuable target tied to health, safety, comfort, access, communication, or something the person actually values.

Long-term questions to consider

  • What daily tasks most affect this person's health and safety?
  • Which skills give them access to things they genuinely value?
  • How do they communicate pain, refusal, preference, fatigue, or confusion during care?
  • What routines would another caregiver need to learn if the current caregiver became unavailable?
  • Which abilities should continue being practiced after school services end?
  • If a skill becomes harder with aging, sensory change, illness, or disability progression, can the support adapt without threatening the person's home?
  • Are we teaching skills to improve this person's life—or to make their life look more conventional?

Casa de SAM's position: the skill serves the life

Casa de SAM believes life skills matter.

We also believe independence is not the price of adulthood.

Residents should have opportunities to learn, participate, communicate, grow, and gain abilities throughout life. But a resident who needs help dressing at 25, bathing at 45, or eating at 70 has not failed.

The purpose of support is not to prove how little support a person can survive with. It is to help build a life that is safe, known, dignified, and genuinely theirs.

The skill serves the life. The life does not exist to demonstrate the skill.

About Casa de SAM

Casa de SAM is a developing vision for a lifelong residential community in Paraguay for adults with autism and other developmental disabilities who need substantial daily support. The project is being designed around safety, flexible daily rhythms, meaningful choice, relationships, and belonging that does not have to be earned through productivity or independence.

Casa de SAM is not yet an operating nonprofit or residential provider. We are currently researching, documenting the vision, and looking for people who may eventually help build it responsibly.

This article provides general educational information and describes Casa de SAM's developing philosophy. It is not individualized medical, therapeutic, educational, behavioral, or program-placement advice. Skills and supports should be selected according to the individual's health, communication, preferences, abilities, risks, and changing needs.
