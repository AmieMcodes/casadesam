LEGAL RIGHTS AND ADULT DECISION-MAKING · OCTOBER 9, 2026

Guardianship, Supported Decision-Making, and Adult Disability

A practical guide to legal authority, less restrictive supports, and the questions families should ask before anyone loses a right.

A parent's question often sounds simple: "When my disabled child becomes an adult, how will I keep helping?" The legal answer is rarely one-size-fits-all. Needing help with medication, banking, transportation, or daily care does not automatically mean that another person should control every legal decision.

Guardianship, supported decision-making, powers of attorney, and benefit-specific arrangements solve different problems. Which tools are available depends on the person's circumstances and the law where the decision is made. A family should begin with the particular decision and the particular support required, not with a label or diagnosis.

This is especially important for people who communicate without speech or who need continuous physical assistance. The ability to live independently and the legal right to participate in decisions are not the same thing. Supporting Choice When an Adult Cannot Live Completely Independently explores that distinction in ordinary daily life.

Start by separating assistance from authority

Consider an adult who needs someone to arrange medical appointments and explain what is happening. One question is whether the person can understand and express a decision with accessible information, time, communication assistance, or trusted support. A second question is who may legally consent if, for a particular decision, the person cannot make or communicate it under the applicable law. A third question is who will actually provide the care afterward.

Those are three jobs, and they may belong to different people. A helpful parent may be a supporter without automatically being an authorized medical decision-maker. A court-appointed representative might have specified powers without being the best person to explain an option or provide hands-on care. A residential provider may coordinate treatment but cannot assume legal authority merely because the adult lives there.

In the United States, the Department of Justice's overview of less restrictive options describes supported decision-making, advance planning, limited legal arrangements, and representative payees as distinct possibilities. It urges consideration of options that preserve rights before seeking broad guardianship.

What guardianship can do, and what it can take away

In U.S. jurisdictions that use guardianship, a court may appoint someone to make specified personal decisions for an adult found to meet that jurisdiction's legal standard. Financial powers may be handled under a separate conservatorship or guardianship of the estate. Names, procedures, evidentiary standards, and retained rights vary across states.

This can be a necessary protection when an adult faces serious decisions they cannot manage even with appropriate assistance. Families confronting repeated medical emergencies, exploitation, or complex care should not be shamed for asking how lawful representation works.

But guardianship is not a routine birthday form. It can remove important rights. The National Council on Disability's 2018 report documents serious concerns about loss of self-determination and access to less restrictive alternatives. Its work is a policy analysis, not proof that every guardianship is unnecessary.

A responsible legal inquiry asks: Which specific decisions cannot safely be handled with support? What evidence establishes that? Could a narrower order meet the actual need? How are the person's views documented? What review and restoration mechanisms exist?

What supported decision-making actually means

Supported decision-making (SDM) is a process in which the disabled person makes their own decisions using trusted assistance. Supporters might explain choices, compare consequences, gather records with proper permission, help with communication, or organize a meeting. The supporter does not acquire a general right to overrule the person.

The U.S. Administration for Community Living emphasizes that support can be tailored by subject: someone may need help with finances but not friendships, or with medical information but not where to spend Saturday.

It is important not to oversell the model. A signed agreement is not magic, and support cannot be used to manufacture an answer the person has not expressed. If a high-stakes decision remains unresolved after appropriate communication and support, families need a lawful path for that specific situation. The legal requirements differ by jurisdiction.

Supported decision-making also requires safeguards. A person should be able to disagree with a supporter, involve someone else, and get help if that supporter is coercive or financially self-interested.

A concrete U.S. example: Texas

Texas Estates Code Chapter 1357 provides for voluntary supported decision-making agreements for qualifying adults with disabilities. A supporter may help the adult understand information and communicate a decision, but may not make it for them. The statute also sets confidentiality and conflict-of-interest obligations.

Texas is an example, not a template that automatically applies elsewhere. Its agreement has eligibility and execution rules, and a household should not assume that signing a downloaded form creates medical, financial, or guardianship powers. Texas also provides for limited or full guardianship through a court where the relevant legal findings are made; the governor's disability guidance explains the requirement to consider available supports and less restrictive alternatives.

Paraguay changed its legal framework in 2025

This subject is especially relevant to Casa de SAM, which is being developed in Paraguay. According to Paraguay's Supreme Court and Judicial Branch, Law No. 7371/2025 took effect July 16, 2025. It changed the Civil Code and judicial framework, replacing traditional disability-related interdiction, declarations of incapacity, and curatorship with individualized supports intended to respect the person's will and preferences.

This is not simply an American debate transplanted to Paraguay. Local lawyers, courts, service organizations, and future residential providers must work with the new Paraguayan model and its evolving implementation. A U.S. guardianship order or Texas support agreement should never be treated as automatically controlling a decision in Paraguay. Cross-border families need advice in each relevant jurisdiction.

The underlying rights principle is also expressed in Article 12 of the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities: disabled people have legal capacity on an equal basis with others, and support arrangements must include safeguards against conflicts of interest and undue influence. How countries implement that commitment still differs.

Choose tools by the job they actually perform

Rather than searching for one document to cover everything, make a simple inventory:

  • Daily choices: Can the adult express preferences through speech, pictures, AAC, gestures, repeated choices, or another method? What assistance improves understanding without pressuring them?
  • Health care: What is the adult able to decide with support? Who may receive medical information or provide consent when legally required? What hospital documents will actually be recognized?
  • Money and benefits: Who can help build a budget, pay bills, or manage public benefits? In the United States, an SSA representative payee manages specified Social Security payments only; this appointment does not grant general medical or property authority.
  • Housing and services: Who signs an agreement, who coordinates care, and who monitors quality? A provider's practical role does not automatically give it legal decision-making power.
  • Safety and complaints: Who can raise concerns independently, and how can the adult replace a supporter or challenge a decision?

The last question matters as much as the first four. A legal arrangement that looks protective on paper can become harmful if no one checks for coercion, abuse, or simply an adult's changing preferences.

What You Can Do This Week

  • Write down five real decisions your family expects in the next year: one medical, one financial, one housing/service, one personal, and one emergency decision.
  • For each, label whether the adult makes it independently, makes it with help, or needs further assessment of support and legal requirements. Do not turn this worksheet into a diagnosis.
  • Try one supported choice using the person's preferred communication method, and record what was offered, how long you waited, and how the response was interpreted.
  • Find the current official rules for the state or country where the decision will occur; save the source link, not a social-media summary.
  • Prepare three concrete questions for a local disability-rights lawyer or advocacy organization, including which less restrictive supports must be considered first.

Long-Term Questions to Consider

Who will help the adult evaluate a new supporter? What happens if a supporter dies or a family member moves abroad? Which legal arrangements, if any, will remain valid after a change of residence or country? How will medical, housing, and financial decisions be reviewed as abilities and needs change? Who is independent enough to challenge a conflict of interest?

A Parent's Guide to Planning for Lifelong Disability Care offers a broader way to organize those questions before a crisis.

Casa de SAM's Position

Casa de SAM's founding vision begins with the resident's voice: their direct choices, communication style, repeated preferences, comfort, distress, and long-term wishes. It calls for following the proper legal authority when required, while never treating family concern as a substitute for hearing the adult.

We are not claiming that every person with profound support needs can make every decision without a legal representative. We are saying that the question deserves an individualized, rights-respecting answer, with safeguards that remain meaningful after the original caregiver is gone. Casa de SAM is still a developing project, not an operating residential program.

About Casa de SAM

Casa de SAM is a developing vision for a lifelong residential community in Paraguay for adults with developmental disabilities who need substantial daily support. Its founding principles center safety, dignity, joyful daily life, and lasting belonging after full acceptance.

Casa de SAM is not yet an operating nonprofit or residential provider. We are researching and developing the legal, financial, staffing, and oversight structures needed to build the vision responsibly.
